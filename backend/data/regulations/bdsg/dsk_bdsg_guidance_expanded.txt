bdsg DSK DPO Appointment
Title: DPO appointment threshold — practical interpretation of "20 persons constantly engaged"
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Non-public bodies where 20 or more persons are permanently engaged in automated personal data processing
Text: The DSK (Datenschutzkonferenz) and BfDI (Bundesbeauftragte für den Datenschutz) have clarified the interpretation of BDSG §38(1) in multiple guidance documents. The threshold requires counting all persons who are "ständig mit der automatisierten Verarbeitung personenbezogener Daten beschäftigt" — permanently engaged in automated processing of personal data. This includes all employees who regularly use computer systems (CRM, ERP, email, HR software, databases, cloud platforms) that process personal data, regardless of whether data processing is their primary job function. Part-time employees, temporary workers (Leiharbeitnehmer), and freelancers who regularly access systems containing personal data all count toward the threshold. Seasonal fluctuations do not matter — the assessment is based on normal business operations ("in der Regel"). Employees who only occasionally touch personal data (e.g. a warehouse worker who once per month logs a signature in a delivery system) generally do not count. In practice, for most digital-first or service businesses, virtually all office workers count because they use email, CRM, or HR systems daily. The DPO must have expert knowledge of data protection law and practice (§38 BDSG, Art. 37(5) GDPR). The DPO may be internal or external (§38(2) BDSG). Internal DPOs enjoy special dismissal protection (§38(2) BDSG referencing §6(4) BDSG) — they cannot be dismissed or disadvantaged because of their DPO activities. The DPO must be provided with necessary resources, access to processing operations, and time. Companies should document their assessment of whether the 20-person threshold is met, retaining the analysis for supervisory authority inquiries.
Keywords: DPO threshold, 20 persons, ständig beschäftigt, Datenschutzbeauftragter, BDSG §38, BfDI guidance, DSK, DPO appointment, dismissal protection, automated processing count

bdsg DSK Employee Monitoring
Title: Employee monitoring — limits on surveillance, email/internet monitoring, and video surveillance
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All employers who monitor employees or employee communications in Germany
Text: The DSK has published extensive guidance on permissible employee monitoring under BDSG §26 in conjunction with GDPR. Employers may not conduct blanket monitoring of email, internet usage, or telephone calls. Private use policies matter — if private use of work email or internet is permitted or tolerated, the employer is treated as a telecommunications provider and must comply with telecommunications secrecy (Art. 10 GG, §3 TDDDG). This significantly restricts monitoring capabilities. If private use is prohibited and this prohibition is actively enforced, the employer retains greater rights to monitor business communications for legitimate purposes (performance, compliance, security). Video surveillance of workplaces is regulated by BDSG §4 (for publicly accessible areas) and §26(1) (for non-public workplace areas). Continuous monitoring of employee workstations is prohibited as disproportionate. Video surveillance is only permitted where there is a concrete security need (theft prevention in warehouses, access control in sensitive areas) and less intrusive alternatives have been considered. Covert surveillance is only permissible for investigating specific documented criminal suspicions and only after weighing proportionality. GPS tracking of company vehicles is permissible only with employee awareness and where necessary for legitimate business operations (fleet management, customer service scheduling) — tracking during private use or breaks is prohibited. All monitoring measures require a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) if they involve systematic, large-scale monitoring of employees.
Keywords: employee monitoring, video surveillance, email monitoring, BDSG §26, telecommunications secrecy, private use, GPS tracking, DPIA, DSK guidance, workplace privacy

bdsg DSK Data Breach Notification
Title: Data breach notification — German-specific procedures and supervisory authority reporting
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers experiencing a personal data breach within Germany
Text: While data breach notification is primarily regulated by Art. 33/34 GDPR, the DSK has published specific guidance for German controllers. A data breach must be reported to the competent state supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware. "Awareness" means the point at which the controller has a reasonable degree of certainty that a security incident has compromised personal data — not the moment an employee first notices an anomaly. The notification must include: nature of the breach (categories of data, approximate number of affected persons, approximate number of data records); name and contact of the DPO; likely consequences; measures taken or proposed. Where the full picture is not yet available within 72 hours, notification must be made with available information and supplemented subsequently (Art. 33(4) GDPR). Documentation of all breaches (including those not requiring notification) must be maintained for supervisory authority review. In Germany, the notification must be made to the supervisory authority of the federal state in which the controller is established (not the BfDI, unless the controller is a federal public body). Most state authorities provide online notification forms. Common breaches requiring notification include: ransomware attacks affecting personal data availability; lost or stolen laptops/USB drives with unencrypted personal data; email misdirection of sensitive data to wrong recipients; database exposure through misconfigured cloud services. Reporting to affected data subjects (Art. 34 GDPR) is required when the breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights — this applies to most breaches involving financial, health, or authentication data.
Keywords: data breach, notification, 72 hours, Art. 33, supervisory authority, DSK guidance, documentation, ransomware, data loss, German notification procedure

bdsg DSK DPIA
Title: Data protection impact assessment — DSK blacklist of processing requiring DPIA in Germany
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Controllers conducting processing operations on the DSK DPIA blacklist or meeting Art. 35(3) GDPR criteria
Text: Under Art. 35(4) GDPR, each EU member state supervisory authority publishes a list of processing activities that mandate a DPIA. The DSK has published the German DPIA blacklist (Muss-Liste), which includes the following processing activities: large-scale profiling of individuals including credit scoring, personality assessment, or behaviour prediction; systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas; processing of biometric data for unique identification in employment contexts; data processing by welfare organisations, debt collection, or insolvency administrators; use of AI or machine learning to make automated decisions about individuals; cross-referencing of personal data from multiple sources for profiling; processing personal data in call centres for marketing or customer analysis; use of telematics systems in vehicles or insurance that track behaviour; creation of comprehensive movement profiles (GPS tracking, telecommunications location data). Beyond this list, a DPIA is always required when processing involves: systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects (profiling); large-scale processing of special category data (Art. 9) or criminal offence data (Art. 10); large-scale systematic monitoring of a publicly accessible area (Art. 35(3) GDPR). The DPIA must contain: systematic description of processing operations; necessity and proportionality assessment; risk assessment for data subjects; measures to address risks. The DPO must be consulted during the DPIA (Art. 35(2) GDPR). The controller must consult the supervisory authority prior to processing if the DPIA indicates high residual risk that cannot be mitigated (Art. 36 GDPR prior consultation).
Keywords: DPIA, Datenschutz-Folgenabschätzung, DSK blacklist, Muss-Liste, risk assessment, profiling, biometric, AI decisions, Art. 35, prior consultation

bdsg BDSG §34-37 Data Subject Rights
Title: Data subject rights under BDSG — German restrictions and sector-specific limitations
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers processing personal data of data subjects in Germany
Text: The BDSG modifies and restricts certain GDPR data subject rights through German opening clauses. Right of access (§34 BDSG): the right of access under Art. 15 GDPR may be restricted where providing the information would involve a disproportionate effort and appropriate technical and organisational measures ensure data protection compliance. The right does not apply to data that is only retained because it cannot be deleted due to statutory or regulatory retention obligations, and where providing the information would require a disproportionate effort. Right to erasure (§35 BDSG): §35 BDSG modifies Art. 17 GDPR — instead of erasure, the controller may restrict processing where erasure would be impossible or involve disproportionate effort due to the specific nature of the storage (e.g. backup tapes). However, this restriction must be documented and the data subject must be informed. Right to data portability (Art. 20 GDPR): no BDSG modification — applies as per GDPR. However, the DSK has noted that the data portability right applies only to data provided by the data subject and processed based on consent or contract — it does not extend to derived or inferred data. Right to object (§36 BDSG): the right to object under Art. 21 GDPR is modified — the controller may continue processing where it can demonstrate compelling legitimate grounds that override the data subject's interests, rights and freedoms, or where processing is necessary for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims. Automated individual decision-making (§37 BDSG): automated decisions producing legal effects may be permissible without consent where necessary for a contract, authorised by law, or where appropriate safeguards are in place.
Keywords: data subject rights, BDSG §34, §35, §36, §37, right of access, right to erasure, data portability, right to object, automated decisions, German restrictions, opening clauses
