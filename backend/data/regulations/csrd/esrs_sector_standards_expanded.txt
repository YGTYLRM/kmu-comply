European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) — Topical Standards
EFRAG, adopted by EU Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2772
Applicable under CSRD Directive 2022/2464/EU
Large companies: reporting periods from 1 January 2024
SMEs listed: from 1 January 2026 (voluntary VSME standard available)

esrs1_double_materiality
Title: Double materiality assessment — financial and impact materiality, methodology requirement
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All companies in scope of CSRD — first step before determining which standards apply
Text: The double materiality assessment (DMA) is the foundational process that determines which ESRS topics are material for a specific company and therefore which disclosures are required. A topic is material under ESRS 1 if it meets either or both of two dimensions. Financial materiality: the topic represents a risk or opportunity that has or could have material influence on the company's cash flows, development, performance, position, cost of capital, or access to finance in the short, medium, or long term — this is the perspective of financial statement materiality (inside-out from the enterprise). Impact materiality: the topic relates to actual or potential significant positive or negative impacts of the company on people or the environment (employees, communities, ecosystems, climate) in the company's own operations or value chain — this is the perspective of sustainability impact (outside-in from society). Assessment process: the company must systematically identify sustainability matters, assess their significance for each materiality dimension, consult with stakeholders (employees, affected communities, value chain partners, users of sustainability reporting), document the assessment methodology and results, and disclose the outcome (which topics are material, which are not, and why). Material topics trigger detailed disclosures under the relevant topical ESRS standards. Non-material topics may be omitted with a brief explanation of the materiality rationale. The DMA must be reviewed at least annually and updated to reflect changes in the company's situation, value chain, or regulatory context. External assurance is required for the DMA process and results from 2024.
Keywords: double materiality, financial materiality, impact materiality, ESRS 1, DMA, stakeholder consultation, outside-in, inside-out, topical standards, materiality assessment, value chain, assurance

esrs1_value_chain_scope
Title: Value chain scope of ESRS disclosures — upstream, own operations, downstream
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All companies reporting under CSRD — determines scope of data collection
Text: ESRS 1 requires that sustainability disclosures cover the company's full value chain including upstream value chain (suppliers of raw materials, components, and services), own operations (all entities included in the consolidated financial statements scope), and downstream value chain (distributors, retailers, and end consumers of the company's products and services, as well as product use and end-of-life). The comprehensiveness of value chain reporting is subject to a proportionality principle — companies are expected to extend data collection efforts into the value chain in proportion to the materiality and the practical feasibility of obtaining data. Where value chain data is not available, companies may use estimates, sector averages, proxy data, or modelled data with appropriate disclosure of the estimation methodology. For the first years of reporting (transitional relief): Scope 3 GHG emissions and other value chain data may benefit from phase-in provisions allowing simplified disclosure in years 1-3 while companies develop data collection capabilities. The value chain scope is particularly important for: Scope 3 GHG emissions (ESRS E1), human rights due diligence (ESRS S2), environmental pollution from suppliers, and supply chain due diligence (intersection with LkSG for German companies). Companies must map their value chain, identify key impacts and dependencies in each tier, and develop a data collection strategy including supplier questionnaires, industry databases, and third-party data providers.
Keywords: value chain, upstream, downstream, own operations, Scope 3, supplier data, proportionality, ESRS 1, data collection, estimation methodology, transitional relief, CSRD reporting scope

esrs_e1_climate_governance
Title: ESRS E1 — Governance of climate-related risks and opportunities
Obligation: MUST (if climate is material)
Threshold: Companies for which climate change is a material topic (most large companies)
Text: ESRS E1 requires disclosure of governance arrangements for climate-related risks and opportunities aligned with the TCFD (Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures) framework now embedded in ESRS. Governance disclosures must cover: board-level oversight — identification of the board committee or individual board member responsible for climate oversight; frequency of board briefings on climate-related topics; how climate performance is incorporated into executive remuneration (if applicable). Management roles — identification of management functions responsible for day-to-day climate risk management; reporting lines to the board; integration with enterprise risk management (ERM) processes. Climate scenario analysis: companies must conduct climate scenario analysis covering both physical risks (acute and chronic physical impacts of climate change) and transition risks (policy, market, technology, reputation risks from transition to a low-carbon economy). Scenarios must include at minimum a 1.5°C aligned scenario (Paris Agreement scenario) and a scenario based on current policies. The analysis must cover the short-term (0-1 year), medium-term (1-5 years), and long-term (5+ years) time horizons. Results of scenario analysis must be integrated into the company's strategic planning and reflected in its transition plan. Companies not yet conducting formal climate scenario analysis should disclose this limitation and provide a timeline for implementation. The ESRS E1 governance disclosure is one of the mandatory ESRS 2 cross-cutting requirements regardless of materiality outcome.
Keywords: ESRS E1, climate governance, TCFD, board oversight, management roles, climate scenario analysis, 1.5°C scenario, physical risk, transition risk, executive remuneration, strategic planning, Paris Agreement

esrs_e1_ghg_emissions
Title: ESRS E1 — GHG emissions disclosure: Scope 1, 2, and 3
Obligation: MUST (if climate is material)
Threshold: Companies for which climate change is material — Scope 1 and 2 always required when climate is material; Scope 3 phased
Text: ESRS E1 requires GHG emissions disclosure following the GHG Protocol methodology (the standard also referenced by CDP, SBTi, and most corporate sustainability reporting frameworks). Scope 1 emissions (direct): GHG emissions from sources owned or controlled by the company including combustion of fuels, industrial processes, company-owned vehicle fleet. Must be disclosed by gas (CO2, CH4, N2O, HFCs, PFCs, SF6, NF3) and by GHG Protocol category. Scope 2 emissions (indirect from purchased energy): emissions from generation of purchased electricity, steam, heating, or cooling consumed by the company; both market-based (using supplier-specific emission factors, renewable energy certificates) and location-based (using grid average emission factors) methods must be disclosed. Scope 3 emissions (indirect value chain): 15 categories of upstream and downstream value chain emissions — the most material Scope 3 categories must be identified through the DMA and disclosed; for most manufacturing companies, categories 1 (purchased goods and services), 11 (use of sold products), and 12 (end-of-life treatment) are typically most significant. Total GHG emissions in CO2 equivalents must be disclosed. Intensity metrics: at least one sector-relevant GHG intensity metric (e.g. tCO2e per €M revenue, per tonne of product, per m² of building) must be disclosed. Gross and net emissions must be reported separately — carbon offsets must not be presented as emissions reductions but disclosed separately. GHG targets: companies with Paris-aligned science-based targets (SBTi validated) must disclose them; companies without targets must explain their approach to target-setting.
Keywords: ESRS E1, Scope 1, Scope 2, Scope 3, GHG emissions, CO2 equivalent, GHG Protocol, market-based, location-based, renewable energy, carbon offset, intensity metric, science-based targets, SBTi

esrs_e1_transition_plan
Title: ESRS E1 — Climate transition plan disclosures
Obligation: MUST (mandatory cross-cutting under ESRS 2 if material)
Threshold: All large companies in CSRD scope — transition plan or explanation of absence required
Text: ESRS E1 requires disclosure of whether the company has adopted a climate transition plan for climate change mitigation. If adopted, the transition plan must cover: decarbonisation targets — GHG reduction targets for 2025, 2030, and 2050 aligned with the Paris Agreement (1.5°C pathway); actions and measures — specific initiatives to achieve the targets including energy efficiency investments, renewable energy procurement, process electrification, supply chain decarbonisation; capital expenditure (CapEx) and operational expenditure (OpEx) allocated to climate transition; locked-in GHG emissions — from existing assets (long-life capital-intensive assets that will continue to emit during their useful life); fossil fuel exposure — revenues, assets, and expenditures related to coal, oil, and gas activities; financial risks from stranded assets. If the company does not have a transition plan, it must disclose this fact and provide a timeline and process for developing one. Transition plan disclosure is directly linked to the EU Taxonomy regulation — companies reporting under CSRD must also disclose EU Taxonomy alignment (proportion of CapEx, OpEx, and revenue aligned with the EU Taxonomy for Sustainable Finance). The transition plan must be approved by the management body and reviewed at least annually. Companies with science-based targets (SBTi-validated 1.5°C targets) should cross-reference their SBTi commitment in the transition plan disclosure. Transition plans that are credible and well-evidenced significantly reduce regulatory and litigation risk.
Keywords: ESRS E1, transition plan, decarbonisation targets, 2030 targets, net zero, CapEx OpEx, locked-in emissions, stranded assets, fossil fuel exposure, EU Taxonomy, science-based targets, Paris Agreement

esrs_e1_physical_risks
Title: ESRS E1 — Physical climate risk assessment and adaptation measures
Obligation: MUST (if climate is material)
Threshold: Companies with material climate-related physical risks — particularly relevant for manufacturing, real estate, agriculture, and infrastructure
Text: ESRS E1 requires disclosure of how the company assesses and manages physical climate risks. Physical risks are classified as: acute (discrete, event-driven climate hazards such as extreme precipitation, flooding, storms, heatwaves, wildfires, droughts) and chronic (longer-term shifts in climate patterns such as temperature increase, sea level rise, changing precipitation patterns, ocean acidification). Assessment process: companies must identify which physical hazards are relevant to their locations and value chain; assess the vulnerability of their assets, operations, and supply chain to identified hazards under different climate scenarios (1.5°C, 2°C, current policies); estimate the financial impact of physical risks (asset damage, business interruption, supply chain disruption, insurance cost increases). Adaptation measures: companies must disclose measures they have implemented or plan to implement to adapt to physical climate risks including: asset hardening (flood barriers, cooling systems, structural reinforcement), operational adaptations (diversified supply chains, relocation of exposed assets), financial measures (insurance, hedging of commodity price risk from climate impacts). Disclosure must include: the identified acute and chronic hazards; location of exposed assets or operations; estimated financial impact or range; adaptation measures implemented; residual risk after adaptation. For German companies: flood risk (Rhine, Elbe, local waterways) is a significant physical risk for many locations; increased summer temperatures affect manufacturing productivity and data centre cooling.
Keywords: ESRS E1, physical climate risk, acute risk, chronic risk, flooding, heatwave, climate hazard, adaptation measures, scenario analysis, asset exposure, business interruption, supply chain risk, financial impact

esrs_s1_workforce_disclosures
Title: ESRS S1 — Own workforce — headcount, contracts, and gender metrics
Obligation: MUST (if own workforce is material — typically always material for employers)
Threshold: All companies in CSRD scope with their own employees
Text: ESRS S1 requires comprehensive disclosures about a company's own workforce (employees and non-employee workers). Headcount disclosures: total headcount at period end by employment type (permanent vs fixed-term contracts), working time (full-time vs part-time), by gender (female, male, other/not disclosed), by country if significant. Non-employee workers: total number of non-employee workers whose work is directed by the company (agency workers, freelancers, self-employed persons on-site) must also be disclosed. Geographic distribution: headcount by country for each country where the company has significant operations or workforce. Management diversity: percentage of women in senior management positions (company board, executive committee level, all management); targets for management gender balance if set. Pay gap: gender pay gap — ratio of female to male median hourly pay — must be disclosed; this is a mandatory ESRS S1 datapoint; methodology must be consistent with the EU Pay Transparency Directive calculation methodology. Collective bargaining: percentage of employees covered by collective bargaining agreements (Tarifvertrag) must be disclosed; social dialogue mechanisms (works council — Betriebsrat — coverage) must be described. Turnover: employee turnover rate (voluntary and involuntary separately) for the reporting period. Training: average training hours per employee per year. Safety: work-related accidents, fatalities, lost time injury rate (LTIR) — see ESRS S1 health and safety requirements.
Keywords: ESRS S1, own workforce, headcount, gender pay gap, full-time, part-time, fixed-term, permanent, collective bargaining, Tarifvertrag, management diversity, non-employee workers, turnover, training hours

esrs_s1_working_conditions
Title: ESRS S1 — Working conditions, health and safety performance indicators
Obligation: MUST (if material)
Threshold: All companies with employees — health and safety typically always material
Text: ESRS S1 requires disclosure of working conditions policies and health and safety performance. Working conditions policies: description of policies covering working time compliance, adequate remuneration (above minimum wage), work-life balance, remote work arrangements, and protection of workers in atypical forms of employment. Health and safety management system: description of the H&S management system (ISO 45001 or equivalent); how the H&S policy is communicated to and implemented by employees; worker involvement in H&S decision-making; hazard identification and risk assessment process (Gefährdungsbeurteilung for German companies). Performance indicators (mandatory ESRS S1 datapoints): number of work-related fatalities (employees and non-employees); number of recordable work-related accidents; lost time injury rate (LTIR) — number of lost-time injuries per 1,000,000 hours worked; rate of work-related ill health — number of cases of recordable work-related ill health per 1,000,000 hours worked; number of days lost to work-related accidents and ill health. Gender-disaggregated H&S data should be provided where available. Occupational diseases: number and type of occupational disease (Berufskrankheit) cases recognised during the reporting period. For German companies: data should be consistent with DGUV (Deutsche Gesetzliche Unfallversicherung) reporting requirements and the Berufsgenossenschaft accident statistics, which provide a reliable data source for these ESRS datapoints.
Keywords: ESRS S1, health and safety, LTIR, lost time injury rate, fatalities, recordable accidents, occupational disease, ISO 45001, Gefährdungsbeurteilung, DGUV, Berufsgenossenschaft, working conditions, minimum wage

esrs_s1_equal_treatment
Title: ESRS S1 — Equal treatment, opportunities, and measures against discrimination
Obligation: MUST (if material)
Threshold: Companies with employees — typically material for all large employers
Text: ESRS S1 requires disclosure of the company's approach to equal treatment and non-discrimination. Policy disclosures: description of policies covering non-discrimination and equal opportunities in recruitment, promotion, remuneration, training, and disciplinary procedures; how policies are communicated and enforced; grievance and complaint mechanisms for discrimination cases. Gender pay equity: beyond the gender pay gap metric (ratio of median pay), companies should disclose measures taken to address unexplained pay gaps; whether pay equity audits are conducted; outcomes of pay equity reviews. Equal opportunities initiatives: programmes to promote diversity in recruitment, including measures to increase representation of underrepresented groups; targets and progress. Parental leave: return-to-work rate after parental leave (Elternzeit) by gender; average duration of parental leave taken by gender. Training and development equity: whether access to training, mentoring, and career development opportunities is monitored for equity across gender, age groups, and other protected characteristics. AGG compliance (German employers): the ESRS S1 disclosures directly relate to AGG obligations — companies should align their AGG §12 preventive measures and §13 complaints procedure with ESRS S1 disclosures for consistency and efficiency in compliance reporting. The EU Pay Transparency Directive (to be transposed by Germany by June 2026) will require more detailed pay transparency measures that will feed into ESRS S1 disclosures.
Keywords: ESRS S1, equal treatment, non-discrimination, gender pay gap, parental leave, return to work, diversity, inclusion, pay equity, AGG, Pay Transparency Directive, recruitment, training equity

esrs_s2_value_chain_workers
Title: ESRS S2 — Workers in the value chain — due diligence and impacts
Obligation: MUST (if value chain worker rights are material)
Threshold: Companies with supply chains involving significant human rights risks (manufacturing, textiles, agriculture, electronics)
Text: ESRS S2 addresses the impacts of the company on workers in its value chain — both upstream (supplier workers, including those of indirect suppliers) and downstream (workers involved in distribution and retail of company products). Material topics covered: child labour; forced labour; freedom of association and collective bargaining; safe and healthy working conditions; adequate wages; hours of work; access to remedy for work-related grievances. Policy disclosures: description of due diligence policies for value chain worker rights; how policies are implemented and communicated to suppliers; how supplier compliance is monitored and enforced; grievance mechanisms accessible to value chain workers. Intersection with LkSG (German companies): for companies with ≥1,000 employees, ESRS S2 disclosures will largely overlap with LkSG reporting requirements — companies should integrate LkSG compliance evidence into ESRS S2 disclosures to avoid duplicate effort. Risk assessment process: identification of geographies and industries in the supply chain with elevated human rights risk; assessment methodology (questionnaires, audits, certification schemes); red flags and escalation process. Remediation: how the company responds to identified violations; corrective action plans with suppliers; last-resort supplier termination; remedy provided to affected workers. Targets: companies should set time-bound targets for reducing identified risks in the supply chain.
Keywords: ESRS S2, value chain workers, human rights, child labour, forced labour, freedom of association, living wage, LkSG alignment, supply chain due diligence, grievance mechanism, supplier audit, high-risk geographies

esrs_g1_business_conduct
Title: ESRS G1 — Business conduct — anti-corruption, anti-bribery, and fair competition
Obligation: MUST (governance typically always material)
Threshold: All companies in CSRD scope — business conduct is expected to be material for all large companies
Text: ESRS G1 covers business conduct disclosures including anti-corruption, anti-bribery, lobbying, and fair competition. Anti-corruption and anti-bribery: companies must disclose their anti-corruption and anti-bribery policies (scope, key provisions, training requirements); procedures for reporting suspected corruption (whistleblowing channel, often same as HinSchG channel); investigations and confirmed incidents of corruption during the reporting period (number of investigations, confirmed violations, sanctions). Political engagement and lobbying: disclosure of lobbying activities and political contributions; whether the company operates in countries with elevated corruption risk; membership in industry associations engaged in lobbying on sustainability-related topics. Fair competition: policies for compliance with antitrust and competition law; confirmed incidents of anti-competitive behaviour during the reporting period (cartel investigations, fines). Payment practices: average payment period to suppliers; percentage of invoices paid beyond agreed payment terms; disclosure relevant for SME suppliers — companies should disclose their supplier payment policies and compliance, particularly for SMEs (the EU Late Payments Regulation imposes mandatory 30-day payment terms for B2B transactions). Supplier relationship practices: description of how the company manages supplier relationships including fair tendering processes, prevention of abuse of superior bargaining power, and transparent contract terms. For German companies: the GwG anti-money laundering compliance and the HinSchG whistleblower channel should be referenced in ESRS G1 disclosures as they are directly relevant to anti-corruption governance.
Keywords: ESRS G1, anti-corruption, anti-bribery, lobbying, political contributions, fair competition, antitrust, payment practices, supplier relationships, whistleblowing, GwG, HinSchG, business conduct

esrs_materiality_phased_requirements
Title: ESRS phase-in and transitional provisions — what can be omitted in early years
Obligation: CONDITIONAL (phase-in applies to specific datapoints in first reporting years)
Threshold: Companies reporting under CSRD for the first time in 2024, 2025, or 2026
Text: ESRS 1 includes important transitional provisions that reduce the disclosure burden for companies in their first years of CSRD reporting. Year 1 (reporting period starting 1 January 2024 — large PIEs): companies may omit certain Scope 3 GHG emission categories where data is not available, provided they disclose the omission and a timeline for inclusion; biodiversity and ecosystem-related datapoints (ESRS E4) may be omitted in year 1 with explanation; certain ESRS S1, S2, S3, S4 value chain datapoints may be omitted in year 1. Year 1 and Year 2 (for companies with fewer than 750 employees): specific numeric datapoints in ESRS S1 (employee data) may benefit from additional phase-in. Years 1-3 (phased Scope 3): full Scope 3 GHG emissions by all 15 categories required from year 3; earlier years may focus on the most material Scope 3 categories identified in the DMA. VSME standard: for SMEs listed on regulated markets (in scope from 2026) and for unlisted SMEs voluntarily reporting, EFRAG has developed the VSME (Voluntary SME) standard as a simplified alternative to the full cross-sectoral ESRS. The VSME has two modules — a basic module (Module B) covering the minimum expected disclosures and a narrative (Module N) for more qualitative information. Large companies should be aware that their supply chain data requests to SME suppliers should ideally be aligned with the VSME standard rather than demanding full ESRS-level disclosures from smaller suppliers.
Keywords: ESRS transitional provisions, phase-in, Scope 3 phase-in, VSME, SME standard, year 1 omissions, reporting relief, first year CSRD, biodiversity phase-in, employee data phase-in, supply chain data requests

esrs_assurance_and_penalties
Title: ESRS assurance requirements and CSRD non-compliance penalties
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All companies in CSRD scope
Text: CSRD requires external assurance of sustainability reporting — this is a significant change from voluntary sustainability reporting where assurance was optional. Assurance requirements: from the first reporting period (2024 for large PIEs): limited assurance required from a statutory auditor or independent assurance service provider; from reporting periods starting 1 January 2028 (or earlier if EU Commission adopts earlier mandate): reasonable assurance required. Assurance scope: the assurance engagement covers the whole of the sustainability statement included in the management report; assurance providers must assess whether disclosures comply with ESRS requirements, the double materiality assessment process, and the tagging of information in the European Single Electronic Format (ESEF/XBRL). Auditor competence: statutory auditors providing CSRD assurance must meet competence requirements specified by IESBA and member state professional bodies; in Germany, this is regulated through the Wirtschaftsprüferkammer. CSRD integration with financial reporting: the sustainability statement is a mandatory part of the management report (Lagebericht) — it is filed with the German Federal Gazette (Bundesanzeiger) alongside the financial statements. Non-compliance penalties under German UDRG (CSRD transposition): CSRD was transposed into German law via the UDRG (Umsetzungsgesetz zur CSRD) in 2024; penalties for false or materially misleading sustainability reporting and for failure to publish the sustainability statement are subject to the same enforcement regime as financial reporting violations; supervisory authority is the Deutsche Prüfstelle für Rechnungslegung (DPR) and BaFin for listed companies.
Keywords: CSRD assurance, limited assurance, reasonable assurance, statutory auditor, ESEF, XBRL, Wirtschaftsprüferkammer, UDRG, sustainability statement, Lagebericht, Bundesanzeiger, DPR, BaFin, CSRD penalties
