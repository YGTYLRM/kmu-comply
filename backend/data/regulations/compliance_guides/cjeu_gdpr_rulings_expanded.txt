cjeu_schrems_ii_c311_18
Title: Schrems II (C-311/18) — Privacy Shield invalid; SCCs require Transfer Impact Assessment
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers and processors transferring personal data outside the EEA
Text: The Schrems II judgment of 16 July 2020 is the most consequential GDPR ruling for international data transfers. The CJEU invalidated the EU-US Privacy Shield adequacy decision, finding that US surveillance law (particularly FISA §702 and Executive Order 12333) does not provide an equivalent level of protection to EU fundamental rights. Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) remain a valid transfer mechanism under Article 46(2)(c) GDPR, but the Court held that parties relying on SCCs must conduct a case-by-case Transfer Impact Assessment (TIA) before transfer to assess whether the destination country's law allows public authorities to access transferred data in ways incompatible with EU fundamental rights. Where the TIA reveals that SCCs alone cannot ensure adequate protection, the exporter must implement supplementary technical, contractual, or organisational measures — or must suspend or not commence the transfer. Practical consequences for German companies: every data transfer to the US and other non-adequate countries must be assessed; US cloud services, SaaS, and analytics tools are particularly affected; encryption of data before transfer (with keys held exclusively in the EU) is the primary supplementary technical measure; the EU Data Privacy Framework (DPF), adopted July 2023, provides a new adequacy decision for US companies self-certified under the DPF — reliance on DPF requires checking the US company appears on the DPF list at dataprivacyframework.gov. A Schrems III challenge to the DPF is considered likely given Schrems I and II history — controllers should not assume the DPF is permanent.
Keywords: Schrems II, C-311/18, Privacy Shield, Standard Contractual Clauses, SCC, Transfer Impact Assessment, TIA, US data transfer, FISA, surveillance law, EU Data Privacy Framework, adequacy, EEA transfers, Article 46

cjeu_planet49_c673_17
Title: Planet49 (C-673/17) — pre-ticked checkboxes invalid; scrolling not consent
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers using cookies or similar tracking technologies on websites
Text: The Planet49 judgment of 1 October 2019 authoritatively establishes what constitutes valid consent for cookies and similar technologies under ePrivacy/GDPR. The CJEU held: (1) A pre-ticked checkbox does not constitute valid consent — the user must actively check the box to give consent; silence, inactivity, or pre-selection cannot constitute consent. (2) Consent must be as easy to withdraw as to give — if consent is given by ticking a box, withdrawal must be equally simple. (3) The fact that a user participates in a promotional game and proceeds by clicking a button is not sufficient evidence of freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent if consent to cookies is bundled with participation in the game (bundling prohibition). (4) Information about the duration of cookies and whether third parties have access must be provided for consent to be fully informed — a general reference to a cookie policy without specifying this information is insufficient. Application to TTDSG/TDDDG (German ePrivacy): §25 TDDDG (formerly TTDSG) requires prior informed consent before accessing or storing information on users' terminal equipment (including cookies) unless strictly necessary for a service explicitly requested by the user. The Planet49 consent standard applies directly. Practical implications: cookie banners must present genuine accept and reject options with equal prominence; pre-ticked consent for analytics or advertising cookies is invalid; continued browsing or scrolling does not constitute consent; cookie banner walls (access to content conditional on accepting cookies) are generally unlawful under German DSK guidance.
Keywords: Planet49, C-673/17, cookie consent, pre-ticked checkbox, §25 TTDSG, TDDDG, bundling prohibition, consent withdrawal, cookie wall, CJEU, ePrivacy, tracking consent, informed consent

cjeu_fashion_id_c40_17
Title: Fashion ID (C-40/17) — embedding social plugins creates joint controllership with plugin provider
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Website operators embedding third-party elements (social buttons, analytics, advertising scripts)
Text: The Fashion ID judgment of 29 July 2019 established that a website operator who embeds a Facebook Like button (or similar third-party social plugin) on their website is a joint controller with Facebook for the collection and transmission of visitor data to Facebook — even if the website operator cannot access or process that data themselves. The CJEU reasoned that by embedding the plugin, the website operator participates in the determination of the means of processing (inclusion of the code that triggers the data collection) and therefore is jointly responsible for the initial collection and transmission, although not for Facebook's subsequent processing. Practical obligations arising from joint controllership (Art. 26 GDPR): a joint controller arrangement must be agreed upon between the parties (in practice, this means the website operator must rely on Facebook's or the plugin provider's published terms as the Art. 26 arrangement); the privacy notice must inform users about the joint controllership and the respective roles; both joint controllers must ensure a legal basis exists for the processing. Extension of the principle: the Fashion ID logic applies to any embedded third-party element that causes data collection on page load — Google Analytics, Google Tag Manager, Google Fonts loaded from Google servers, LinkedIn Insight Tag, Facebook Pixel, Twitter/X tracking, Hotjar, and similar tools all trigger data collection. For hosted tools (where the code runs on the website operator's servers without phone-home), joint controllership typically does not arise. Website operators should audit all embedded third-party resources and assess joint controllership implications for each.
Keywords: Fashion ID, C-40/17, joint controller, Art. 26 GDPR, Facebook Like button, social plugins, embedded scripts, Google Analytics, tracking, joint controllership arrangement, privacy notice, third-party elements

cjeu_wirtschaftsakademie_c210_16
Title: Wirtschaftsakademie (C-210/16) — Facebook page administrators are joint controllers
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All organisations operating Facebook pages, LinkedIn company pages, or similar social media presences
Text: The Wirtschaftsakademie judgment of 5 June 2018 established that the administrator of a Facebook fan page (Fanpage) is a joint controller with Facebook for the processing of visitor data, including the fan page insights data provided by Facebook to the administrator. The CJEU found that the page administrator contributes to determining the means of processing by choosing to create the page and defining the target audience parameters — even though the administrator cannot control or access the underlying technical processing. Implications: any company operating a Facebook business page, LinkedIn company page, Instagram business account, or similar professional social media presence is a joint controller with the platform provider for visitor data processing. This requires: a legal basis for the joint processing; transparency in the privacy notice about the joint controllership and data flows to the social network; an Art. 26 arrangement (in practice, Meta's page administrator terms serve as this arrangement, but companies must accept these terms having reviewed them for GDPR compliance). Data subject rights: requests relating to fan page insights data or social network visitor data received from data subjects must be forwarded to the platform, as the platform controls the underlying data. Companies cannot rely on their social media presence being operated "by the platform" to avoid data protection responsibility — they have active accountability for the joint processing. DSK guidance (see dsk_kp17_cookies_tracking) explicitly states that German DPAs consider fan page operation to involve joint responsibility.
Keywords: Wirtschaftsakademie, C-210/16, Facebook page, LinkedIn page, fan page, joint controller, Art. 26 GDPR, social media, Instagram, page insights, joint responsibility, privacy notice

cjeu_google_spain_c131_12
Title: Google Spain (C-131/12) — right to erasure / delisting from search engines
Obligation: MUST (for controllers operating search functions or maintaining searchable public indexes)
Threshold: Search engine operators and similar indexing services; right of data subjects applies universally
Text: The Google Spain judgment of 13 May 2014 established the right to erasure (right to be forgotten) as a right to request delisting from search engine results — even where the information at the indexed source remains lawful and accessible. The CJEU held that: Google is a data controller for the processing of personal data involved in indexing and searching; the processing of personal data by a search engine can constitute a significant interference with the rights and freedoms of data subjects; data subjects can request delisting of specific results relating to them from search results; the right must be balanced against the public interest in freedom of information — the balance shifts depending on whether the data subject is a public figure, the nature of the information, and whether it remains accurate and relevant. Under Art. 17 GDPR (codifying and expanding the Google Spain principle): the right to erasure applies across all controllers, not only search engines; grounds for erasure include: data no longer necessary for the purpose; consent withdrawn and no other legal basis; legitimate interest overrides; unlawful processing; erasure required by legal obligation. Refusal of erasure requires one of the Art. 17(3) exceptions (freedom of expression, legal obligation, public interest, legal claims, scientific/historical research). For German companies: erasure requests must be handled within 1 month; reasons for refusal must be communicated; the request must be evaluated per-purpose and per-system (erasure from one system does not automatically cover all systems where the data appears).
Keywords: Google Spain, C-131/12, right to erasure, right to be forgotten, Art. 17 GDPR, search engine delisting, freedom of expression, public interest, erasure request, data controller, 1 month deadline

cjeu_breyer_c582_14
Title: Breyer (C-582/14) — dynamic IP addresses can be personal data
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All website operators and online service providers that log IP addresses
Text: The Breyer judgment of 19 October 2016 addressed whether a dynamic IP address (one that changes with each internet session and is not permanently assigned to a specific person) constitutes personal data within the meaning of the Data Protection Directive (now GDPR). The CJEU held that a dynamic IP address can constitute personal data for the website operator if the operator has the legal means to identify the individual using the address — for example, by requesting identification information from the internet service provider in the context of a criminal investigation. The Court applied a relativist approach: whether data constitutes personal data depends on the means reasonably available to the data controller — if the controller cannot identify the individual (e.g. through legal means that are actually available, not merely theoretical), the data may not be personal data for that controller. Practical application for German companies: most commercial website operators cannot directly identify individuals from dynamic IPs without ISP cooperation (which requires a court order or law enforcement request) — but since this possibility cannot be ruled out in principle, dynamic IP addresses are generally treated as personal data under GDPR by German DPAs. DSK and German courts treat IP address logging as personal data processing requiring a legal basis. Consequence: web server access logs containing IP addresses constitute personal data processing; these logs must have a documented legal basis (typically legitimate interest under Art. 6(1)(f) for IT security purposes), a retention period (typically 7-14 days for standard access logs), and must be mentioned in the privacy notice.
Keywords: Breyer, C-582/14, dynamic IP address, personal data, relativist approach, website operator, access log, ISP, identification, legal means available, GDPR personal data definition

cjeu_schufa_c634_21
Title: SCHUFA scoring (C-634/21) — automated credit scoring can be automated decision-making under Art. 22
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Controllers using automated scoring or profiling in high-stakes decisions (credit, employment, insurance)
Text: The SCHUFA Holding judgment of 7 December 2023 addressed whether credit scoring by credit bureaus falls within Article 22 GDPR's prohibition on solely automated decisions. The CJEU held that automated credit scoring by a credit bureau constitutes a form of automated decision-making subject to Article 22 where the score plays a decisive role in the decision made by the third party receiving the score — even if the third party (bank) technically takes the final credit decision. The judgment confirms that the analysis must look at the overall decision-making process, not just the final step. If the credit bureau score effectively determines the outcome, Article 22 applies to the credit bureau's scoring process. Data subject rights under Art. 22 GDPR: where Art. 22 applies, data subjects have the right to: not be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing; obtain meaningful information about the logic involved (Art. 15(1)(h) right of access); request human review of the automated decision; express their point of view; contest the decision. Practical implications for German companies: any automated scoring or profiling system used to make or significantly influence decisions about individuals (credit approval, insurance premiums, job screening, tenant screening) must be assessed against Art. 22; where Art. 22 applies, a legal basis is required (explicit consent, contractual necessity, or specific law); the privacy notice must explain the automated decision-making and its logic; a human review process must be available and genuinely accessible. SCHUFA and other credit bureaus are required to provide access to scoring methodology under Art. 15 GDPR following this judgment.
Keywords: SCHUFA, C-634/21, automated decision-making, Art. 22 GDPR, credit scoring, profiling, right to human review, explainability, Art. 15 GDPR, automated processing, sole automated decision

cjeu_meta_legitimate_interest_c252_21
Title: Meta Platforms (C-252/21) — dominant market position weakens legitimate interest for personalised advertising
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Controllers using legitimate interest as the legal basis for data processing for advertising or profiling
Text: The Meta Platforms judgment of 4 July 2023 addressed the data processing practices of Facebook for personalised advertising. The CJEU provided important guidance on the scope of legitimate interest as a legal basis and on consent in monopoly/oligopoly market conditions. On legitimate interest: a company's dominant market position is relevant to the balancing test — the interests and fundamental rights of data subjects carry greater weight where they have limited alternative services, which makes it harder for legitimate interest to override their privacy interests. On consent in dominated markets: where a data subject has no real alternative to using a service (due to network effects, market dominance, or lock-in), consent to data processing beyond what is necessary for the service may not be freely given within the meaning of Art. 7(4) GDPR. Personalised advertising: processing personal data for behavioural advertising is not strictly necessary for providing the social network service — it requires a genuine legal basis separate from the service contract. Practical implications for German companies: companies should carefully reassess legitimate interest assessments for advertising and profiling use cases; where the company has a strong market position or where users have limited alternatives, legitimate interest is a riskier legal basis; consent for advertising should be genuinely voluntary with a real alternative (service access without personalised advertising). German DPAs and DSK have extended this reasoning to analytics and tracking use cases — the German DPA position is that analytics based on legitimate interest is highly risky without genuine opt-out capability.
Keywords: Meta, C-252/21, legitimate interest, Art. 6(1)(f), dominant market position, personalised advertising, consent, freely given, network effects, balancing test, profiling, social network

cjeu_damage_compensation_c300_21
Title: UI v Oesterreichische Post (C-300/21) — GDPR infringement alone does not guarantee compensation
Obligation: SHOULD (risk management — damages exposure calibration)
Threshold: All controllers — relevant for understanding litigation risk
Text: The UI v Österreichische Post judgment of 4 May 2023 addressed when compensation is payable under Article 82 GDPR for non-material damage (immaterial harm) resulting from a GDPR infringement. The CJEU held: (1) A mere infringement of the GDPR does not automatically give rise to a right to compensation — there must be actual material or non-material damage. (2) Non-material damage can include emotional distress, anxiety, or fear resulting from the infringement, but this must be demonstrated by the data subject; it cannot be purely theoretical or assumed. (3) National courts may not set a de minimis threshold below which non-material damage is not compensated — if damage is proven, however small, it is compensable. (4) The three conditions for compensation are: GDPR infringement, damage (material or non-material), and causal link between infringement and damage — all must be proven by the claimant. Practical implications: mass data breach litigation in Germany and EU is growing — class action-style coordinated claims (e.g. via consumer protection organisations) are becoming more common; while individual damages per claimant may be modest (courts have awarded €100-€5,000 for emotional distress in individual cases), the cumulative exposure from large data breaches involving many data subjects is significant; controllers should treat data minimisation and security measures as key risk mitigation tools; prompt and transparent breach response reduces reputational damage and may reduce litigation exposure; documenting that appropriate security measures were in place is important evidence in litigation defence.
Keywords: C-300/21, Art. 82 GDPR, data breach compensation, non-material damage, immaterial harm, emotional distress, GDPR litigation, three conditions, causal link, class action, damages Germany

cjeu_working_time_recording_c55_18
Title: CJEU Working Time Recording (C-55/18) — employers must measure daily working time of each worker
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All employers in Germany with employees — applies under ArbZG
Text: The CCOO v Deutsche Bank judgment of 14 May 2019 interpreted the Working Time Directive 2003/88/EC and found that member states must require employers to set up an objective, reliable, and accessible system enabling the duration of time worked each day by each worker to be measured. While this ruling predates widespread GDPR enforcement discussion, it is directly relevant for German compliance because: (1) German labour courts have applied this ruling to interpret ArbZG — employers must maintain working time records for all employees sufficient to demonstrate compliance with maximum working time, rest period, and break requirements; the ruling effectively extends the ArbZG documentation obligation to all employees, not just those explicitly covered by the narrow ArbZG §17 provision. (2) Working time records constitute personal data (name, start time, end time, duration of work for specific individuals) — their collection, processing, storage, and retention must comply with GDPR and BDSG §26 (employee data). Retention: working time records under ArbZG must be retained for 2 years; additional retention may apply under MiLoG §17 (also 2 years, but the covered group and format differ). (3) Employee monitoring concerns: implementing working time recording systems (time clocks, timesheets, app-based recording) requires works council co-determination (§87(1) Nr. 6 BetrVG) where a works council exists; the chosen method must be proportionate and the least privacy-intrusive approach sufficient for the compliance purpose.
Keywords: CCOO, C-55/18, working time recording, ArbZG, working time directive, employer obligation, time records, all employees, works council, §87 BetrVG, personal data, GDPR employee records

cjeu_right_of_access_nowak_c434_16
Title: Nowak (C-434/16) — examination scripts and internal assessments are personal data
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers maintaining records that include evaluations or assessments of natural persons
Text: The Nowak judgment of 20 December 2017 addressed whether handwritten examination answers constitute personal data of the candidate. The CJEU held that the answers written by a candidate in a professional examination do constitute personal data of the candidate because they contain information about their knowledge, competence, and intellectual performance. The examiner's comments on the script also constitute personal data of the candidate because they reflect the examiner's opinion about the candidate's performance. Implications for German companies: interview notes, performance review notes, assessment centre evaluations, job application screening records, competency assessments — all constitute personal data of the assessed individual and are subject to GDPR. Data subject rights apply: candidates and employees have the right of access (Art. 15 GDPR) to their interview notes and performance assessments; refusal of access is only permissible under the narrow Art. 15(4) and BDSG §37 exceptions; where access is refused, reasons must be given. Retention: assessment records should be retained for the minimum period necessary — for unsuccessful job applicants, German practice is to retain for 6 months post-decision (covering potential AGG discrimination claims). For current employee performance records, retention as part of the personnel file for the duration of employment plus 3-5 years is typical. Companies should include internal assessment records in their ROPA and ensure these are covered in data subject rights procedures.
Keywords: Nowak, C-434/16, examination scripts, personal data, interview notes, performance review, right of access, Art. 15 GDPR, BDSG §37, assessment records, job applicants, employee records

cjeu_rynes_c212_13
Title: Rynes (C-212/13) — home CCTV covering public space is subject to GDPR
Obligation: MUST (for employers operating surveillance cameras)
Threshold: Controllers operating video surveillance covering areas beyond the immediate private sphere
Text: The Rynes judgment of 11 December 2014 established that a home CCTV system that captures images of a public street or a neighbour's property falls outside the household exemption of data protection law and is subject to full data protection obligations. While this ruling predates GDPR, the principle applies under Art. 2(2)(c) GDPR (personal/household activity exception). For German companies operating video surveillance: workplace surveillance cameras are subject to GDPR, BDSG §26 (employee data), and applicable state data protection law; a legal basis under Art. 6(1) GDPR and specifically for employee data under BDSG §26(1) is required; DSK has published specific guidance on video surveillance (see dsk_kp18_video_surveillance); a DPIA under Art. 35 GDPR is typically required for systematic monitoring of employees or public-accessible premises; camera placement, retention periods, and access controls must be documented; where a works council exists, video surveillance requires co-determination under §87(1) Nr. 6 BetrVG; signage informing data subjects of surveillance is mandatory; images must be deleted within the minimum necessary period — typically 48-72 hours unless a specific incident requires longer retention (maximum 10 days for most commercial purposes in German practice). Access to surveillance footage must be restricted to authorised persons.
Keywords: Rynes, C-212/13, CCTV, video surveillance, household exemption, Art. 2(2)(c), workplace surveillance, Art. 35 DPIA, works council, BetrVG, signage, retention, employee monitoring
