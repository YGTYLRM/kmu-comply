dsk_kp1_controller_processor
Title: Who is controller vs processor — joint controllers under Art. 26 GDPR
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Any organisation processing personal data; all companies operating in Germany under GDPR scope
Text: The Datenschutzkonferenz (DSK) — the body of German federal and state data protection authorities — has issued guidance clarifying the distinction between controller (Verantwortlicher) and processor (Auftragsverarbeiter) under Arts. 4(7) and 4(8) GDPR. The controller is the natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of processing. The processor processes personal data only on behalf of the controller and strictly according to the controller's instructions. The DSK's practical test asks: who decides why data is collected and what it is used for? Whoever answers that question is the controller, regardless of whether they physically possess the data. A cloud service that processes data only on customer instruction is a processor; a SaaS platform that exploits the same data for its own analytics purposes may be a joint controller or independent controller. Joint controllers under Art. 26 GDPR arise whenever two or more controllers jointly determine purposes and means. The DSK emphasises that joint controllership does not require a formal agreement — the factual decision-making pattern is decisive. However, Art. 26(1) requires joint controllers to determine their respective responsibilities in a transparent arrangement, typically a joint controller agreement. The arrangement must cover which party handles data subject rights requests, which party provides Art. 13/14 notices, and how liability is allocated. Data subjects must be able to exercise their rights against any of the joint controllers. Controllers and processors must document their relationship in a data processing agreement (DPA) under Art. 28 GDPR; a DPA cannot substitute for a joint controller agreement where joint controllership exists.
Keywords: controller, Verantwortlicher, processor, Auftragsverarbeiter, joint controller, gemeinsam Verantwortliche, Art. 26 GDPR, DPA, data processing agreement, DSK, purposes and means

dsk_kp2_legal_bases
Title: The six legal bases under Art. 6 GDPR — DSK guidance on which applies when
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers processing personal data; each processing activity requires a valid legal basis before processing begins
Text: Article 6 GDPR provides an exhaustive list of six legal bases for processing personal data. The DSK has issued guidance on how to apply each correctly. (1) Consent (Art. 6(1)(a)): Must meet the requirements of Art. 7 — freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous. Consent cannot be the general fallback and should only be used where the data subject has a genuine choice. (2) Contract (Art. 6(1)(b)): Processing must be strictly necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or for pre-contractual steps at their request. The DSK warns against overly broad interpretations — processing must be objectively necessary, not merely useful. (3) Legal obligation (Art. 6(1)(c)): The obligation must be imposed by EU or member state law, not by contractual or commercial necessity. This covers tax record-keeping, occupational health and safety documentation, and statutory employment records. (4) Vital interests (Art. 6(1)(d)): Limited to life-threatening situations; not available for ordinary commercial processing. (5) Public task (Art. 6(1)(e)): Reserved for public authorities and bodies carrying out their official duties; generally not available to private companies. (6) Legitimate interests (Art. 6(1)(f)): Available to private controllers but requires a three-step Legitimate Interest Assessment (LIA) — purpose identification, necessity test, and balancing against data subject rights. The DSK emphasises that the legal basis must be identified before processing begins, documented in the ROPA, and communicated to data subjects in the Art. 13/14 notice. Switching legal bases retrospectively is not permitted. Recital 50 GDPR confirms that further processing for incompatible purposes cannot be legitimised simply by invoking a different legal basis from the one used at collection.
Keywords: legal basis, Rechtsgrundlage, Art. 6 GDPR, consent, Einwilligung, contract, Vertrag, legal obligation, legitimate interest, berechtigtes Interesse, DSK, six bases, LIA

dsk_kp3_consent_requirements
Title: Consent under Art. 7 GDPR — freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous; DSK position on cookie walls
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Any controller relying on consent as legal basis under Art. 6(1)(a) or Art. 9(2)(a) GDPR
Text: The DSK has comprehensively addressed consent requirements in multiple Kurzpapiere and orientations, closely aligned with the EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 on consent. Consent under Art. 4(11) and Art. 7 GDPR must be: (a) Freely given — data subjects must have a genuine choice and suffer no detriment for refusing or withdrawing consent. Where there is a clear imbalance of power (employment context), consent is presumed not to be freely given. (b) Specific — consent must be sought separately for each distinct processing purpose; bundling multiple purposes into a single consent violates the specificity requirement. (c) Informed — data subjects must receive the Art. 13/14 information before giving consent; consent obtained without adequate information is invalid. (d) Unambiguous — consent requires a clear affirmative act. Silence, pre-ticked boxes, inactivity, or scrolling through a webpage do not constitute valid consent, as confirmed by the CJEU in Planet49 (C-673/17). Withdrawal of consent must be as easy as giving it — a one-click opt-out must be available wherever a one-click opt-in was used. The bundling prohibition (Art. 7(4)) means that conditioning a service on consent to unnecessary processing makes consent unfree. On cookie walls, the DSK has taken the position that blocking access to a website unless the user accepts non-essential tracking cookies renders consent unfree because access to the service is conditional on consent. Controllers must record evidence of consent, including when and what information was provided. Consent records must be retained as long as processing based on consent continues. Parental consent for children under 16 is required for information society services (no lower age threshold has been set in German law — §8 BDSG uses 16 years). The TTDSG §25 governs cookie consent specifically (see dsk_kp17_cookies_tracking).
Keywords: consent, Einwilligung, freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, pre-ticked boxes, withdrawal, cookie wall, bundling prohibition, Art. 7 GDPR, Planet49, DSK, EDPB

dsk_kp4_transparency
Title: Transparency obligations — Art. 13 and 14 GDPR notice requirements; layered notices
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers; Art. 13 applies when data collected directly from data subject; Art. 14 when obtained from third parties
Text: The DSK's transparency guidance addresses the mandatory content of privacy notices under Arts. 13 and 14 GDPR. For data collected directly (Art. 13), the controller must provide at the time of collection: identity and contact details of the controller; contact details of the DPO (if appointed); processing purposes and legal basis for each purpose; legitimate interests pursued (if Art. 6(1)(f) applies); recipients or categories of recipients; international transfer information; retention periods or criteria; data subject rights (access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, objection); right to withdraw consent (if applicable); right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority; whether provision of data is statutory/contractual and consequences of non-provision; existence of automated decision-making including profiling and logic involved. For data obtained indirectly (Art. 14), the same information applies plus the source of the data. Art. 14 notices must be provided within one month, or at first contact with the data subject, or at disclosure if earlier. The DSK endorses layered notices as a practical solution for digital contexts — a short first layer provides the most important information with a clear link to the full second layer. Layered notices are acceptable provided the full information is genuinely accessible and not concealed. Notices must be in plain language understandable to the target audience; technical or legal jargon must be avoided. Children's services must use age-appropriate language. Notices must be freely accessible, not hidden in terms and conditions. Controllers must update notices when processing changes and re-notify data subjects if the change materially affects them.
Keywords: transparency, Datenschutzerklarung, privacy notice, Art. 13 GDPR, Art. 14 GDPR, layered notice, mandatory content, DPO, data subject rights, plain language, controller identity, DSK

dsk_kp5_ropa
Title: Records of Processing Activities (ROPA) under Art. 30 GDPR — DSK guidance; applies to all organisations
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: DSK position: all organisations regardless of employee count, since most process data non-occasionally; Art. 30(5) exemption for <250 employees is narrow
Text: Article 30 GDPR requires controllers and processors to maintain records of processing activities (ROPA — Verzeichnis von Verarbeitungstaetigkeiten). The statutory exemption under Art. 30(5) for organisations with fewer than 250 employees applies only where the processing does not carry a risk for the rights and freedoms of data subjects, is not occasional, and does not include special categories or criminal conviction data. The DSK has taken the firm position that most organisations — including small businesses — process data non-occasionally (they regularly process employee data, customer data, and supplier data on an ongoing basis) and therefore cannot rely on the Art. 30(5) exemption. In practice, the DSK expects all organisations to maintain a ROPA. The controller ROPA must contain: name and contact details of the controller and DPO; processing purposes; categories of data subjects and personal data; categories of recipients; third-country transfers with documentation of safeguards; retention periods or deletion criteria; general description of technical and organisational security measures (TOMs). The processor ROPA must contain: name and contact details of the processor and controllers for whom it acts; categories of processing per controller; cross-border transfers; TOMs. The ROPA must be maintained in writing, including electronic form. It must be made available to the supervisory authority on request. The DSK recommends reviewing and updating the ROPA at least annually and whenever a new processing activity is introduced or an existing one changes materially. A ROPA that is not up to date is a compliance deficit and may indicate non-compliance with the accountability principle under Art. 5(2).
Keywords: ROPA, Verarbeitungsverzeichnis, records of processing activities, Art. 30 GDPR, 250 employees exemption, DSK, all organisations, controller ROPA, processor ROPA, accountability, annual review

dsk_kp6_tom_security
Title: Technical and organisational measures (TOMs) under Art. 32 GDPR — pseudonymisation, encryption, resilience; must be specifically documented
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers and processors; measures must be appropriate to the risk of the processing
Text: Article 32 GDPR requires controllers and processors to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures (TOMs — technisch-organisatorische Massnahmen) to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk. The DSK guidance specifies that TOMs must be documented concretely — generic statements such as "appropriate security measures are in place" are not compliant. The documentation must name the specific measures implemented for each processing activity or category of processing. Art. 32(1) lists as examples: (a) Pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data — the DSK recommends state-of-the-art encryption for data at rest and in transit; pseudonymisation is specifically listed as a means to reduce risk but does not render data outside GDPR scope; (b) Ensuring ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience of processing systems — this encompasses access controls, backup systems, redundant infrastructure, and business continuity provisions; (c) Ability to restore availability and access to personal data in a timely manner in the event of an incident — RTO and RPO should be defined for systems containing personal data; (d) Regular testing, assessing and evaluating the effectiveness of TOMs — the DSK requires penetration tests, vulnerability scans, and security audits at appropriate intervals, with results documented. When selecting TOMs, controllers must consider the state of the art, implementation costs, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of processing, as well as the likelihood and severity of risks. The DSK warns that cost alone does not justify inadequate security — where processing is high-risk, the cost of security measures cannot be balanced against data subjects' rights. A data processing agreement under Art. 28 must specify which TOMs the processor applies; controllers must verify these before engaging a processor and periodically thereafter.
Keywords: TOMs, technisch-organisatorische Massnahmen, Art. 32 GDPR, encryption, pseudonymisation, resilience, availability, security measures, documentation, penetration test, access control, DSK

dsk_kp7_breach_notification
Title: Personal data breach notification — Art. 33/34 GDPR; 72-hour clock; risk assessment; internal register
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers (Art. 33 to supervisory authority; Art. 34 to data subjects); processors must notify controllers without undue delay under Art. 33(2)
Text: Articles 33 and 34 GDPR establish the personal data breach notification framework. The DSK guidance provides critical clarification on the starting point of the 72-hour clock under Art. 33(1): the clock starts when a responsible person within the organisation — not necessarily the DPO or a senior executive — becomes aware of a breach. Awareness of a low-level IT employee or front-line staff member triggers the clock if that person's knowledge can be attributed to the organisation. Controllers must therefore have internal escalation procedures that rapidly bring breach information to the attention of those responsible for GDPR compliance. A breach that is not reported to the supervisory authority within 72 hours must be accompanied by reasons for the delay. Notifications to the supervisory authority (Art. 33) must include: nature of the breach; categories and approximate number of data subjects and records affected; likely consequences; measures taken or proposed to address the breach. If all information is not yet available, the notification may be made in phases. Art. 34 requires direct notification to affected data subjects where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms — a risk threshold higher than Art. 33's general risk threshold. The risk assessment must consider confidentiality, integrity, and availability impacts; types of data involved; number of data subjects; ease of identification; and special categories. Low-risk breaches — those unlikely to result in any risk — need not be reported to the supervisory authority but must still be recorded in the internal breach register under Art. 33(5). The internal register must document all breaches, including those not reported, with facts, effects, and remedial actions. This register is subject to supervisory authority inspection.
Keywords: data breach, Datenpanne, Art. 33 GDPR, Art. 34 GDPR, 72 hours, supervisory authority notification, breach register, risk assessment, high risk, notification obligation, DSK

dsk_kp8_dpia
Title: Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) under Art. 35 GDPR — DSK criteria for high risk; prior consultation
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Controllers where processing is likely to result in high risk; all controllers must screen all new processing activities against DPIA criteria
Text: Article 35 GDPR requires a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA — Datenschutz-Folgenabschaetzung) before commencing processing that is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. The DSK has published criteria expanding on the Art. 29 WP Guidelines 248 rev.01. The DSK's criteria for mandatory DPIA include: systematic and extensive profiling or automated decision-making with significant effects; large-scale processing of special category or criminal conviction data; systematic monitoring of public areas; use of new technologies (e.g. biometrics, AI systems, machine learning); processing that prevents data subjects from exercising a right or using a service or contract; processing that matches or combines datasets from multiple sources in ways data subjects would not expect; processing of vulnerable data subjects (employees, children, patients). A DPIA is required when at least two of the DSK's criteria apply, consistent with EDPB guidance. The DPIA must contain: systematic description of the processing (purposes, legitimate interests if applicable); necessity and proportionality assessment; risk assessment to rights and freedoms; measures to address risks. The controller must consult the competent supervisory authority before commencing processing where the DPIA indicates that residual high risk cannot be mitigated (Art. 36 prior consultation). The supervisory authority has up to 8 weeks to respond (extendable by a further 6 weeks for complex cases). If a DPO has been appointed, they must be consulted in the DPIA process. The DPIA must be reviewed when there is a change to the processing that could affect the risk profile. Each German state DPA publishes a blacklist (Muss-Liste) of processing types always requiring a DPIA under Art. 35(4).
Keywords: DPIA, Datenschutz-Folgenabschaetzung, Art. 35 GDPR, high risk, prior consultation, Art. 36 GDPR, supervisory authority, profiling, new technologies, Muss-Liste, DSK, blacklist

dsk_kp9_dpo
Title: DPO appointment obligation — BDSG §38; 20 persons threshold; internal or external DPO; registration
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Private sector organisations regularly employing 20 or more persons in automated personal data processing; organisations processing special categories or data for profiling at large scale
Text: The obligation to appoint a Data Protection Officer (DPO — Datenschutzbeauftragter) in Germany is governed by both Art. 37 GDPR and §38 BDSG 2018. Section 38(1) BDSG supplements Art. 37 GDPR with a specific German threshold: a controller or processor must appoint a DPO if it regularly employs at least 20 persons who are constantly engaged in the automated processing of personal data. The DSK has clarified that "constantly" (ständig) does not mean without interruption — it means on a regular and recurring basis as part of their normal duties. Employees who process personal data as an incidental part of their role on an irregular basis are not counted. The 20-person threshold counts individuals, not full-time equivalents, and includes part-time staff if their role involves regular automated processing. Separately, a DPO is mandatory under Art. 37(1)(b) GDPR for processing of special category data at large scale, or under Art. 37(1)(c) for large-scale systematic monitoring. An internal DPO must have the expert knowledge, professional reliability, and organisational position required — they must not receive instructions regarding their DPO functions, must be involved in all data protection matters, and must report directly to the highest management level. An external DPO is expressly permitted and can serve multiple organisations. The DPO's contact details must be published on the organisation's website and registered with the competent supervisory authority (in Germany, registration is made via the relevant state DPA's web portal). The DPO must be consulted on DPIAs, breach notifications, and before introducing new processing activities. Organisations may voluntarily appoint a DPO even below the thresholds; if they do, the same legal protections apply.
Keywords: DPO, Datenschutzbeauftragter, BDSG §38, 20 persons, constantly engaged, automated processing, internal DPO, external DPO, registration, supervisory authority, Art. 37 GDPR, DSK

dsk_kp10_rights
Title: Data subject rights under Arts. 15–22 GDPR — response deadlines, identity verification, refusal grounds, fees
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers; obligations apply to all data subjects whose personal data is processed
Text: Articles 15–22 GDPR establish the core data subject rights. The DSK guidance on exercise and fulfilment of these rights is as follows. Response deadlines: controllers must respond within one calendar month of receiving a request. The deadline may be extended by a further two months where requests are complex or numerous; the data subject must be informed of the extension within the initial one-month period with reasons. The clock starts when the request is received, not when identity is verified. Identity verification: controllers may request additional information to verify identity where there is reasonable doubt, but verification requirements must be proportionate. For online services, asking a user to re-authenticate in their account is typically sufficient. Demanding notarised identity documents for routine requests is disproportionate. If the controller cannot identify the data subject, it should not process the request but must communicate this. Right of access (Art. 15): right to a free copy of all personal data; first copy is free, further copies may be subject to a reasonable fee. Charging fees for a first copy is expressly prohibited. The DSK has confirmed that emails, HR records, and IT system logs all fall within Art. 15 scope. Right to erasure (Art. 17): applies where data is no longer necessary, consent withdrawn, data unlawfully processed, or legal obligation requires erasure. Exceptions include legal retention obligations and freedom of information. Right to data portability (Art. 20): applies to data processed by automated means on basis of consent or contract; machine-readable format required (e.g. JSON, CSV). Right to object (Art. 21): data subjects may object to processing based on legitimate interests or for direct marketing at any time; direct marketing objections must be honoured immediately without balancing. Charging fees for exercising rights is only permissible for manifestly unfounded or excessive requests — the controller bears the burden of demonstrating this.
Keywords: data subject rights, Betroffenenrechte, Art. 15 GDPR, Art. 17 GDPR, Art. 20 GDPR, Art. 21 GDPR, one month deadline, identity verification, right of access, erasure, portability, objection, fees, DSK

dsk_kp11_employee_data
Title: Employee data processing — BDSG §26; monitoring; GPS tracking; email monitoring; biometric data; consent in employment
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All employers processing personal data of employees, applicants, and former employees; BDSG §26 is lex specialis for employment context
Text: Section 26 BDSG is the primary legal basis for employee data processing in Germany, implementing the opening clause of Art. 88 GDPR. Under §26(1) BDSG, personal data of employees may be processed for employment relationship purposes when necessary for the decision to establish, execute, or terminate an employment contract. Data necessary for payroll administration, attendance management, or performance management of the employment relationship falls clearly within §26(1). The DSK has issued guidance on specific monitoring practices: GPS tracking of company vehicles is permissible where limited to working hours, proportionate to the legitimate purpose (e.g. route optimisation or safety), and employees are informed. Permanent location tracking outside working hours requires specific consent or very strong justification. Email monitoring: content-based monitoring of employee emails is generally impermissible unless specific grounds exist (suspected serious misconduct); metadata monitoring (sender, recipient, size) may be permissible for IT security purposes with proportionality limitations. CCTV in workplaces must comply with Art. 35 DPIA requirements (systematic monitoring) and is subject to co-determination rights of the works council (Betriebsrat) under BetrVG §87(1) Nr. 6. Biometric data of employees is special category data under Art. 9 GDPR — processing requires an explicit legal basis; in employment context, a collective agreement (Tarifvertrag or Betriebsvereinbarung) may provide the required GDPR opening clause. Consent in employment: the DSK adopts the EDPB position that consent under Art. 6(1)(a) is generally not freely given in employment relationships due to the power imbalance. Employers relying on employee consent should use it only for optional, genuinely discretionary processing (e.g. employee photo for intranet profile) with a documented absence of detriment for refusal.
Keywords: employee data, Beschaeftigtendatenschutz, BDSG §26, employment relationship, GPS tracking, email monitoring, CCTV, biometric data, works council, Betriebsrat, consent in employment, power imbalance, DSK

dsk_kp12_third_country_transfers
Title: Third-country data transfers — post-Schrems II obligations; SCCs plus TIA; supplementary measures; US Data Privacy Framework
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Any controller or processor transferring personal data to a country outside the EEA without an adequacy decision; Arts. 44–49 GDPR apply
Text: Following the CJEU judgment in Schrems II (C-311/18, 16 July 2020), the DSK issued guidance on third-country transfers that remains central to German enforcement. The EU-US Privacy Shield was invalidated; the EU Commission adopted the EU-US Data Privacy Framework (DPF) as an adequacy decision on 10 July 2023, restoring a transfer mechanism for certified US organisations. The adequacy decision does not cover all US entities — only those certified under the DPF. For transfers to non-adequate countries using Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs, adopted by EU Commission 4 June 2021), the DSK requires a Transfer Impact Assessment (TIA — Transferfolgenabschaetzung) for each transfer or category of transfers. The TIA must assess: the legal framework of the destination country (government access laws, surveillance programmes, judicial remedies for EU persons); practical circumstances of the transfer (nature of data, likelihood of government access); available supplementary measures. If the TIA reveals that the destination country's laws prevent effective compliance with the SCCs — meaning government access to the data cannot be excluded — supplementary measures are required. Effective supplementary measures include: end-to-end encryption where the service provider holds no keys; pseudonymisation with keys retained in the EEA; split-processing architectures. Where no effective supplementary measures can be identified, the transfer must be suspended. The DSK has taken a firm enforcement stance: transfers to US cloud providers without a valid TIA and either DPF coverage or supplementary measures are non-compliant. German DPAs have issued fines for transfers where Google Analytics, Facebook pixels, and similar tools transmitted IP addresses to the US without a valid transfer mechanism.
Keywords: third-country transfer, Drittlandubermittlung, Schrems II, SCCs, standard contractual clauses, TIA, Transferfolgenabschaetzung, supplementary measures, US Data Privacy Framework, DPF, adequacy decision, Art. 44 GDPR, DSK

dsk_kp13_legitimate_interest
Title: Legitimate interest assessment (LIA) under Art. 6(1)(f) — three-step test; tracking and profiling exclusions; advertising
Obligation: CONDITIONAL
Threshold: Controllers wishing to rely on legitimate interests as legal basis; LIA must be conducted before processing begins and documented
Text: Article 6(1)(f) GDPR permits processing where necessary for the legitimate interests of the controller or a third party, except where those interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights of the data subject. The DSK's guidance specifies a three-step Legitimate Interest Assessment (LIA — Interessenabwaegung): Step 1 — Purpose: the controller must identify a specific, concrete, and genuine interest. Vague references to "business improvement" or "service personalisation" are insufficient; the interest must be articulated with precision. Step 2 — Necessity: processing must be strictly necessary for the purpose. If a less privacy-invasive means could achieve the same purpose, the necessity test fails. Step 3 — Balancing: the controller must weigh the legitimate interest against the data subject's rights, freedoms, and interests. Relevant factors include: whether the data subject would reasonably expect the processing at the time of collection; the nature of the data; the consequences for data subjects; whether effective opt-out is available. The DSK has specifically addressed high-risk use cases: tracking and profiling of individuals for behavioural advertising cannot rely on legitimate interests because the profiling is disproportionate to user expectations and data subjects cannot reasonably anticipate it at the time of data collection. The DSK's position is aligned with the CJEU ruling in Meta (C-252/21) that personalised advertising based on data from across multiple platforms is not a legitimate interest that overrides data subject rights, particularly for dominant platforms. Direct mail advertising to existing customers may qualify as a legitimate interest (Recital 47 GDPR), subject to the right to object under Art. 21. Legitimate interest cannot be used to circumvent the higher standards for special categories under Art. 9. The LIA documentation must be recorded in writing and attached to or referenced in the ROPA entry.
Keywords: legitimate interest, berechtigtes Interesse, Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR, LIA, Interessenabwaegung, three-step test, balancing, tracking, profiling, advertising, necessity, reasonable expectations, DSK

dsk_kp14_special_categories
Title: Special category data under Art. 9 GDPR — no implicit bases; explicit consent standard; health data in employment
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Any controller processing Art. 9 special category data: health, racial/ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, trade union membership, genetic/biometric data, sex life/orientation, criminal conviction data
Text: Article 9 GDPR prohibits processing of special category data by default, subject to a list of exceptions in Art. 9(2). The DSK guidance emphasises that none of the Art. 9(2) exceptions can be applied by implication or analogy — each must be specifically identifiable. The most commonly invoked exceptions in German practice are: Art. 9(2)(a) — explicit consent: the standard is higher than for ordinary consent; it must be explicit (not inferred from conduct) and specifically directed at the special category processing. Pre-ticked boxes, general terms acceptance, or silence are invalid. Art. 9(2)(b) — employment context: processing necessary for employment, social security, or social protection law obligations, subject to suitable safeguards; implemented in Germany through §26(3) BDSG which permits processing of employee health data where necessary for rights and obligations under employment law. Art. 9(2)(h) — health and social care: processing by health professionals under professional secrecy obligations. Art. 9(2)(j) — scientific/archiving/statistical purposes: subject to Art. 89 safeguards. The DSK has specifically addressed health data in employment: employers may process strictly necessary health data (e.g. fitness for work certificates) without consent where necessary under employment law. However, detailed medical diagnoses or treatment information cannot be demanded; only the conclusion (fit/unfit) is generally necessary. Biometric data used for access control (fingerprint readers, facial recognition) is special category data requiring either explicit consent or a Betriebsvereinbarung (works agreement) under BDSG §26(4) as an alternative legal basis. Genetic data of employees cannot be processed even with consent under most circumstances given the particular sensitivity and irreversibility.
Keywords: special categories, besondere Kategorien, Art. 9 GDPR, health data, biometric data, BDSG §26(3), explicit consent, employment health, Betriebsvereinbarung, genetic data, prohibition, DSK

dsk_kp15_children_data
Title: Children's consent — Art. 8 GDPR; 16-year threshold in Germany (BDSG §8); parental verification; online services directed at children
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Operators of information society services directed at children or potentially accessed by children; Art. 8 GDPR and BDSG §8 apply
Text: Article 8 GDPR regulates consent for information society services directed at children. Member states may lower the age threshold to 13 but not below. Germany has exercised this option in §8(1) BDSG, which sets the threshold at 16 years. Where a child is under 16, consent must be given or authorised by a parent or legal guardian. The DSK guidance addresses the substantial practical challenge of age verification and parental consent verification in online environments. Controllers must make reasonable efforts to verify the age of the data subject and the authority of the parent or guardian, taking into account available technology. The DSK accepts that 100% reliable verification is not technically achievable, but expects proportionate verification steps — for example, collecting a date of birth and cross-referencing it, or requiring parental email confirmation. Services clearly directed at children (e.g. children's gaming, educational platforms) must implement more robust verification than general-purpose services. The standard for "information society services" is broad: any services provided for remuneration over distance and by electronic means, including free services where data is the commercial consideration. The DSK warns that controllers cannot circumvent Art. 8 by asserting that their service is technically open to all ages if in practice it attracts a significant child user base. Privacy notices for child-directed services must be written in age-appropriate language understandable to children. Data minimisation is especially important for children's data — only data strictly necessary for the service may be collected. Profiling of children for commercial purposes is particularly sensitive and the DSK expects it to be avoided unless the controller can establish a compelling justification under Art. 9(2) where special categories are involved.
Keywords: children's data, Kinderdatenschutz, Art. 8 GDPR, BDSG §8, 16 years, parental consent, age verification, information society services, online services, child-directed, DSK

dsk_kp16_profiling_automated_decisions
Title: Profiling and automated decisions — Art. 22 GDPR; prohibition of purely automated significant decisions; right to human review
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Any controller making solely automated decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects on data subjects; includes credit scoring, HR selection, insurance pricing
Text: Article 22 GDPR prohibits subjecting data subjects to solely automated decisions — including profiling — which produce legal effects or similarly significant effects. A decision is "solely automated" when no meaningful human involvement in the decision process occurs; rubber-stamp review does not constitute meaningful human review. The DSK guidance clarifies that legal effects include decisions on credit, employment, social benefits, housing, insurance, and similar high-stakes outcomes. "Similarly significant effects" covers situations with comparable practical consequences — e.g. price discrimination, differential access to services, systematic exclusion from opportunities. The prohibition applies unless: (a) the decision is necessary for the contract (Art. 22(2)(a)) — interpreted narrowly by the DSK; necessity must be genuine and not merely efficient; (b) authorised by EU or member state law with suitable safeguards (Art. 22(2)(b)); (c) explicit consent is obtained (Art. 22(2)(c)). In all permitted cases, the controller must: implement suitable measures to safeguard data subject rights; allow the data subject to obtain human intervention, to express their point of view, and to contest the decision (Art. 22(3)); provide a meaningful explanation of the logic, significance, and envisaged consequences of the automated processing (Art. 15(1)(h)). The DSK's position, aligned with the CJEU judgment in SCHUFA scoring (C-634/21), is that credit bureau scoring systems that effectively determine creditworthiness constitute Art. 22 automated decision-making even where the final decision is nominally made by a human lender who relies almost entirely on the score. Profiling of special category data under Art. 9 requires a separate Art. 9(2) basis in addition to Art. 22 compliance. Controllers using profiling for any purpose must disclose this in Art. 13/14 notices and include it in their ROPA.
Keywords: profiling, automated decision, Art. 22 GDPR, significant effect, legal effect, human review, right to contest, credit scoring, SCHUFA, explanation, Art. 15(1)(h), DSK

dsk_kp17_cookies_tracking
Title: Cookies and tracking technologies — §25 TTDSG (formerly TDDDG); prior consent requirement; reject-all mandatory; cookie walls unlawful; legitimate interest for analytics rejected
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Any operator of a website or app that stores or accesses information on a user's device; §25 TTDSG applies in Germany regardless of GDPR legal basis for subsequent processing
Text: Section 25 TTDSG (Telekommunikation-Telemedien-Datenschutz-Gesetz, in force from 1 December 2021, implementing Art. 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive) requires prior, informed consent before information is stored on or accessed from a user's terminal device, or before any tracking of user behaviour across services is carried out. This consent requirement applies independently of whether the stored or accessed data constitutes personal data under GDPR — the §25 TTDSG requirement is device-focused, not data-focused. Strictly necessary cookies — those essential to the core functionality of a service explicitly requested by the user — are exempt from the consent requirement under §25(2) TTDSG. All other cookies and tracking technologies (analytics, advertising, personalisation, social media pixels, fingerprinting) require prior affirmative consent meeting Art. 7 GDPR standards. The DSK's guidance specifies that a valid cookie consent mechanism must: present an equally prominent "Accept" and "Reject" option; not use dark patterns (pre-selected toggles, confusing language, prominent accept versus buried reject); allow withdrawal of consent as easily as giving it; not condition service access on consent (cookie wall prohibition, consistent with DSK's position under Art. 7(4) GDPR). The DSK has specifically rejected the use of legitimate interests (Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR) as a basis for analytics cookies: the very purpose of §25 TTDSG is to require consent before device access; allowing Art. 6(1)(f) to substitute for §25 consent would undermine the legislative intent. Google Analytics and similar tools therefore require consent unless a privacy-compliant configuration (e.g. IP anonymisation, no cross-site linking, server-side processing in the EEA) can eliminate the §25 trigger. Consent Management Platforms (CMPs) must be configured to collect valid, documented consent; pre-configured CMP templates with pre-selected options are non-compliant.
Keywords: cookies, §25 TTDSG, TDDDG, tracking, ePrivacy Directive, consent, reject-all, cookie wall, dark patterns, analytics, legitimate interest, Google Analytics, CMP, consent management, DSK

dsk_kp18_video_surveillance
Title: Video surveillance of employees and public spaces — proportionality; retention limits; signage; Art. 35 DPIA required for systematic monitoring
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Any controller operating CCTV or video surveillance systems; specific rules under BDSG for employers and BDSG §4 for public areas
Text: Video surveillance (Videoüberwachung) in Germany is regulated by §4 BDSG for publicly accessible spaces and by §26 BDSG read together with Art. 32 and Art. 35 GDPR for employment contexts. For publicly accessible areas (shops, offices, car parks), §4(1) BDSG permits video surveillance only where necessary to safeguard a legitimate interest and there is no reason to assume overriding interests of the persons affected. The controller must make the surveillance visible through clear signage identifying the controller — §4(2) BDSG. The DSK guidance requires: (a) Proportionality: scope of surveillance (covered area, cameras, angles) must not exceed what is necessary for the stated purpose. General prevention of theft or protection of property is a recognised legitimate interest for retail environments; monitoring staff performance, behaviour, or movements requires stronger justification and works council involvement. (b) Retention: footage must generally be deleted within 24–72 hours unless there is a specific incident requiring longer retention for evidentiary purposes; the DSK default is the shortest retention consistent with the security purpose. (c) Signage requirements: signage must be placed before entry to the surveilled area and must identify the controller and provide contact details for data subject rights enquiries. (d) Art. 35 DPIA: systematic monitoring of individuals in an area they cannot avoid entering — including employee surveillance in workplaces — is on the German DPAs' Muss-Listen (mandatory DPIA list). A DPIA must be conducted before implementing workplace CCTV. (e) Works council: under BetrVG §87(1) Nr. 6, introduction of technical systems capable of monitoring employees' conduct or performance requires works council co-determination. Failure to involve the works council renders the surveillance unlawful under employment law irrespective of GDPR compliance.
Keywords: video surveillance, Videoüberwachung, BDSG §4, CCTV, proportionality, retention, signage, Art. 35 DPIA, works council, Betriebsrat, BetrVG §87, employment monitoring, publicly accessible, DSK

dsk_retention_periods_guidance
Title: Data retention periods — DSK guidance; no single correct period; statutory requirements under HGB, AO, ArbSchG; deletion obligations
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers; applies to all processing activities; retention period must be determined per processing purpose and documented in ROPA
Text: The GDPR does not specify fixed retention periods for most categories of personal data; instead, Art. 5(1)(e) requires personal data to be kept in a form that permits identification of data subjects no longer than necessary for the purposes for which it is processed (storage limitation principle). The DSK guidance emphasises that there is no single universally correct retention period — each controller must determine an appropriate period for each processing purpose and document it in the ROPA. The determination process must consider: (1) Statutory retention obligations that override the general data minimisation principle. Key German statutory retention periods include: HGB §257 — commercial books, inventories, balance sheets, accounting records: 10 years; HGB §257 — business correspondence and commercially relevant documents: 6 years; AO §147 — tax-relevant documents (tax returns, invoices, bank records): 10 years; ArbSchG §3 and DGUV Regulations — occupational health and safety records (risk assessments, accident reports): 5 years; working time records under ArbZG: 2 years; §11 MiLoG minimum wage documentation: 2 years; employment contracts and core HR records: generally 3 years after employment end (statute of limitations) plus litigation hold if claims are pending. (2) Contractual and limitation period considerations: under §§195–218 BGB, the standard limitation period is 3 years; for certain commercial claims 10 years (§196 BGB). Data should generally be retained until claims relating to it are time-barred. (3) After the retention period expires, personal data must be deleted or anonymised without delay. Anonymisation — where individuals can no longer be identified even by the controller — removes data from GDPR scope. Controllers must implement technical deletion routines and document their operation; "we forget to delete" is not a valid compliance position. Periodic deletion audits are a DSK recommendation.
Keywords: retention period, Aufbewahrungsfrist, storage limitation, Art. 5(1)(e) GDPR, HGB §257, AO §147, ArbSchG, ArbZG, MiLoG, deletion obligation, ROPA, data minimisation, DSK

dsk_accountability
Title: Accountability principle — Art. 5(2) GDPR; documentation requirements; internal policies; audit trail; demonstrating compliance to supervisory authority
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All controllers; Art. 5(2) GDPR applies to any organisation processing personal data regardless of size, sector, or volume of processing
Text: The accountability principle under Art. 5(2) GDPR requires controllers to be able to demonstrate compliance with the data protection principles in Art. 5(1). The DSK's guidance on accountability provides a practical framework for what "demonstrating compliance" requires in practice. Core documentation elements that constitute the accountability framework: (1) ROPA (Art. 30) — maintained and up to date, covering all processing activities with legal basis, purposes, retention periods, and TOM references. (2) DPO appointment documentation (if applicable under Art. 37/BDSG §38) — appointment letter, registration with DPA, evidence of resources and independence. (3) TOMs documentation (Art. 32) — specific, current, and linked to processing activities. Generic security policies are insufficient; TOMs must be documented per processing context. (4) DPIA records (Art. 35) — conducted for all high-risk processing; reviewed when processing changes; documented with methodology, findings, and mitigation measures. (5) Processor agreements (Art. 28) — DPAs in place with all processors; sub-processing authorisation documented. (6) Training records — evidence that relevant staff have received data protection training appropriate to their role. (7) Breach register (Art. 33(5)) — all breaches recorded, including those below the notification threshold. (8) Consent records — where consent is the legal basis, records demonstrating valid consent was obtained. (9) Data subject request logs — records of all requests received and actions taken, including response times. (10) Third-country transfer documentation — TIAs, SCCs, and supplementary measures for each category of transfer. Internal data protection policies should cover these elements and be approved at senior management level. The DSK recommends a periodic internal audit of the accountability documentation — at minimum annually — to identify gaps before a supervisory authority inspection occurs. During a supervisory authority inspection (Prüfung), controllers must be able to produce all accountability documentation within a short timeframe.
Keywords: accountability, Rechenschaftspflicht, Art. 5(2) GDPR, documentation, ROPA, DPO, TOMs, DPIA, breach register, consent records, audit, supervisory authority inspection, internal policies, data protection audit, DSK
