Datenschutzkonferenz (DSK) and Supervisory Authorities — Cookie Consent and Tracking Guidance
Practical guidance for implementing compliant cookie banners and consent management under
TDDDG §25 (formerly TTDSG §25), in conjunction with GDPR Arts. 6, 7.
Sources: DSK Orientierungshilfe Telemedien 2022, Planet49 ruling (BGH I ZR 7/16), CJEU C-673/17

---

ttdsg DSK Cookie Banner Design
Title: Cookie banner design requirements — DSK guidance on compliant consent mechanisms
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All website and app operators that use non-essential cookies or tracking technologies
Text: The DSK Orientierungshilfe für Anbieter von Telemedien (2022 revision) provides detailed guidance on compliant cookie banner design under TDDDG §25. A compliant cookie banner must: (1) appear before any non-essential cookies are set — no tracking scripts, analytics, or advertising technologies may load until the user has actively consented; (2) provide granular consent options — the user must be able to accept or reject individual categories (analytics, marketing, social media, personalisation) without being forced into an all-or-nothing choice; (3) offer an equally prominent "Reject All" or "Only Essential" button as the "Accept All" button — designs that make the reject option harder to find (smaller text, hidden behind a "Settings" link, different colour) are not compliant; (4) not use dark patterns — pre-ticked checkboxes, deceptive button colours, misleading language ("Recommended: Accept All"), fake closure buttons, or manipulative nudging invalidate consent; (5) provide first-layer information about cookie categories and purposes without requiring the user to navigate to a separate page; (6) allow withdrawal of consent at any time — the mechanism for withdrawal must be as easy as the mechanism for giving consent (e.g. persistent "Cookie Settings" link in the footer). The BGH Planet49 ruling (I ZR 7/16, following CJEU C-673/17) confirmed that pre-ticked checkboxes do not constitute valid consent for cookies under German law. The consent mechanism must result in an active, informed, specific, and unambiguous indication of consent — the GDPR standard applies via TDDDG §25's reference to Art. 7 GDPR.
Keywords: cookie banner, DSK, Orientierungshilfe, consent design, reject button, dark patterns, pre-ticked checkboxes, Planet49, granular consent, first layer, withdrawal

ttdsg Cookie Categories
Title: Cookie classification — essential vs non-essential categories with examples
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All website operators implementing cookie consent management
Text: For compliance with TDDDG §25, cookies and tracking technologies must be classified into categories and treated according to their necessity. Strictly necessary cookies (no consent required): session cookies for shopping carts and form progress; authentication and login session cookies; load balancing cookies; user input cookies (e.g. language selection for the duration of a session); security cookies (CSRF tokens, fraud detection); accessibility preference cookies (font size, contrast) where essential for service delivery. Note: even for strictly necessary cookies, operators should provide transparency via the privacy policy. Consent required — analytics and statistics: Google Analytics, Matomo, Hotjar, Plausible, Mixpanel, Adobe Analytics, or any tool that tracks user behaviour across pages; A/B testing tools; heatmap tools; scroll tracking. Even first-party analytics tools require consent because they access information stored on the user's device. Server-side analytics that do not set cookies or access device storage may be exempt if properly implemented. Consent required — marketing and advertising: Google Ads, Facebook Pixel, LinkedIn Insight Tag, retargeting cookies, conversion tracking, programmatic advertising, affiliate tracking, dynamic pricing based on user behaviour. Consent required — social media: embedded YouTube/Vimeo players with tracking; Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn share buttons that set cookies; comment systems with cross-site tracking (Disqus). Consent required — personalisation: content recommendation engines using device-stored profiles; A/B personalisation; returning visitor recognition using local storage. Third-party services loaded via iframes that set their own cookies require separate consent disclosures.
Keywords: cookie categories, strictly necessary, analytics, marketing, social media, consent required, Google Analytics, Facebook Pixel, session cookies, CSRF, classification

ttdsg Consent Management Platform
Title: Consent management platforms — technical implementation requirements for TDDDG compliance
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All website operators using a consent management platform (CMP) or custom cookie consent solution
Text: A consent management platform (CMP) or custom implementation must technically enforce consent decisions — not merely display a banner. Requirements: (1) No scripts before consent — the page must not load any non-essential third-party scripts, pixels, or tracking technologies until the CMP has received and recorded a valid consent signal. This includes Google Tag Manager containers, Facebook SDK, analytics scripts, and embedded media with tracking. The CMP must act as a gatekeeper, not a notification; (2) Server-side tag firing — if using a tag manager, conditional tag firing based on consent categories must be implemented. Tags without corresponding consent must remain blocked; (3) Consent storage — the consent decision (which categories were accepted/rejected, timestamp, consent version) must be stored in a first-party cookie or local storage. This record serves as evidence of consent for supervisory authority inquiries; (4) Consent renewal — consent should be renewed periodically (DSK recommends at least annually) because the consent must remain current and informed. If the cookie categories or data processors change, re-consent must be obtained; (5) TCF compatibility — for publishers in the programmatic advertising ecosystem, IAB Transparency and Consent Framework (TCF) v2.2 compliance may be relevant, but TCF compliance alone does not guarantee TDDDG compliance as TCF focuses on legitimate interest claims that German supervisory authorities generally reject for tracking; (6) Documentation — operators must be able to demonstrate to supervisory authorities that consent was obtained before tracking began. This requires logging consent events with timestamps and the configuration version active at that time.
Keywords: CMP, consent management platform, tag manager, script blocking, consent storage, consent renewal, TCF, implementation, technical enforcement, documentation

ttdsg Google Analytics Compliance
Title: Google Analytics and US data transfers — TDDDG and GDPR interplay after Schrems II
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: Companies using Google Analytics, Google Tag Manager, or other US-based analytics services
Text: The use of Google Analytics in Germany requires both TDDDG §25 cookie consent AND a valid GDPR legal basis for the associated data transfer to US-based Google servers. Following the Schrems II ruling (CJEU C-311/18) and the subsequent DSK resolutions, German supervisory authorities have taken the position that Google Analytics use without proper consent violates both TDDDG §25 and GDPR Arts. 44-49. The EU-US Data Privacy Framework (DPF, adopted July 2023) provides an adequacy basis for transfers to DPF-certified US companies, including Google. However, operators must still: (1) obtain valid TDDDG §25 consent before loading the Google Analytics script; (2) verify that Google LLC remains DPF-certified for the specific data processing activities; (3) configure Google Analytics with IP anonymisation enabled (anonymize_ip); (4) configure data retention settings to the minimum necessary period; (5) execute a valid data processing agreement with Google; (6) document the legal basis for the transfer in the privacy policy. Austrian, French, and Italian supervisory authorities have issued enforcement decisions finding Google Analytics non-compliant — while these are not directly binding in Germany, they signal the direction of European enforcement. Alternative approaches: server-side analytics proxying (data never leaves the EU); self-hosted analytics (Matomo on EU servers); privacy-focused analytics services hosted exclusively in the EU. Companies should conduct and document a transfer impact assessment (TIA) for any US-based tracking service.
Keywords: Google Analytics, US data transfer, Schrems II, Data Privacy Framework, DSK, IP anonymisation, data processing agreement, transfer impact assessment, Matomo, self-hosted

ttdsg TDDDG §4 Communications Secrecy
Title: Communications secrecy — protection of telecommunications content and metadata
Obligation: MUST
Threshold: All providers of telecommunications services (including employers who provide telephone or email to employees)
Text: TDDDG §3 (formerly TTDSG §3) protects the confidentiality of telecommunications content and circumstances, implementing Art. 10 of the German Basic Law (Grundgesetz). The obligation applies to every provider of telecommunications services — which includes employers who provide telephone, email, or messaging services to employees where private use is permitted or tolerated. Providers may not eavesdrop on, record, or disclose the content of telecommunications without the consent of all participants. Metadata (who communicated with whom, when, duration, location) is equally protected. Exceptions: billing data necessary for service provision; lawful interception under court order (TKG §170 et seq.); network security measures. Practical implications for employers: if private email or phone use is permitted, the employer becomes a telecommunications provider and must comply with communications secrecy — this means the employer generally cannot read employee emails, even business emails, without specific arrangements. The safest approach is either: (a) prohibit private use entirely and actively enforce the prohibition; or (b) obtain explicit, informed employee consent for monitoring specific to each type of communication. Works council agreements (Betriebsvereinbarungen) may provide a framework for balancing employer monitoring rights with employee privacy under §87(1) Nr. 6 BetrVG.
Keywords: communications secrecy, Fernmeldegeheimnis, TDDDG §3, employer telecommunications, email monitoring, private use, metadata, Art. 10 GG, works council
